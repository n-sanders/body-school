Unit 6: Protection and Defense — Integumentary and Immune Systems
Approach

Present the skin as the body’s first physical barrier and the immune system as the specialized defense force that responds when that barrier is crossed. Include the lymphatic system as the circulatory partner of the immune system. Keep the focus on everyday protective functions and basic recognition of major structures.

Key concepts

The integumentary system is the skin and its accessory structures (hair, nails, sweat glands, oil glands).
- Skin has layers: the outer epidermis (barrier, produces new cells) and the deeper dermis (blood vessels, nerves, glands).
- Jobs include protecting against injury and germs, helping regulate body temperature (sweating, blood flow), sensing touch and temperature, and producing vitamin D when exposed to sunlight.
- Hair and nails are modified skin structures that add protection.

The immune and lymphatic systems defend against pathogens (bacteria, viruses, and other invaders) and help clean up damaged cells.
- White blood cells recognize and attack foreign substances. Some produce antibodies that mark invaders for destruction.
- Lymph nodes filter lymph (a fluid that leaks from blood vessels) and house immune cells. The spleen and thymus also play roles.
- The lymphatic vessels return fluid to the blood and transport immune cells.
- Barriers (skin, mucous membranes, stomach acid) are the first line; the immune response is the second line when barriers are breached.

These systems interact with others: the circulatory system carries immune cells, the skin works with the nervous system for sensation, and overall health depends on all systems remaining intact.

Tone guidance for lessons

Start with familiar protective experiences (a scrape healing, feeling cold and shivering, a runny nose when sick) before naming systems.
Distinguish the physical barrier of skin from the active cellular defense of the immune system with clear examples.
Introduce a few key immune players (white blood cells, antibodies, lymph nodes) without requiring detailed immunology.
Note that the course does not cover reproductive anatomy; any brief mention of shared pathways should stay at the level of “some systems share tubes in adults.”
End by preparing students for the final unit, which looks at how all the systems coordinate.
