Unit 4: Fuel and Waste — Digestive and Urinary Systems
Approach

Follow food from the mouth through the digestive tract, showing both mechanical and chemical breakdown and where nutrients are absorbed. Then show how the kidneys filter the blood and remove wastes and excess water as urine. Emphasize that digestion supplies the raw materials cells need while the urinary system keeps the blood’s composition stable.

Key concepts

The digestive system breaks food into molecules the body can absorb and use.
- Digestion begins in the mouth (chewing and saliva) and continues in the stomach (acid and enzymes) and small intestine (most chemical digestion and nutrient absorption).
- Accessory organs help: the liver produces bile (helps digest fats), the gallbladder stores bile, and the pancreas releases enzymes and bicarbonate.
- The large intestine absorbs water and forms feces. Undigested material leaves the body.
- Nutrients (sugars, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals, water) enter the blood or lymph and are carried to cells.

The urinary (excretory) system removes wastes and excess substances from the blood.
- The kidneys filter blood continuously. They reabsorb useful substances and send wastes and extra water into urine.
- Urine travels through the ureters to the bladder, where it is stored, then leaves the body through the urethra.
- The kidneys also help regulate water balance, blood pressure, and the levels of certain salts.

Digestion and excretion are linked through the blood: nutrients absorbed in the small intestine travel in the blood, and the kidneys later clean that same blood of metabolic wastes.

Tone guidance for lessons

Start with a familiar meal and ask what happens to the food after it is swallowed, rather than beginning with a list of organs.
Use simple “journey of a sandwich” or “journey of a glass of water” sequences.
Distinguish mechanical digestion (physical breakdown) from chemical digestion (enzymes and acids) with concrete examples.
Present the kidneys as filters that carefully keep what the body needs and release what it does not, rather than as simple “waste dumps.”
Note that reproductive organs are outside the scope of this course; the urinary and reproductive systems share some pathways in adults but are studied separately here.
End by noting that the nervous and endocrine systems coordinate when and how these processes happen.
