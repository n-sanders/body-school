# Visual teaching in every lesson

Every one of the 42 lessons has a main teaching picture, including introductions and reviews. There are 63 main image placements; Go deeper images are additional. The collection contains 30 Servier PNGs and five retained sourced figures. Eight Servier figures were added in this pass.

## How students use the pictures

Each lesson has a specific **Read the picture(s)** task: compare structures, follow a route, connect a shape to its job, or identify which process the image leaves out. These tasks support independent study and concept-based questions. Labels are reference aids, not vocabulary tests. Several reviews deliberately reuse earlier figures with a new task.

Pictures of organs do not automatically explain feedback, filtration, or immune memory. Captions identify these limits. The separate IMAGE-BACKLOG.md records more focused mechanism diagrams to improve later; every lesson already has useful found imagery now.

## Sources and scope

New figures were downloaded from their official Servier asset pages, visually inspected, retained unmodified, and attributed under CC BY 4.0. Original download URLs and source pages are recorded in image-manifest.json and IMAGE_CREDITS.md. Images stay within the course’s reproductive-anatomy exclusion.

The eight additions are a circulation map, red blood cell, white blood cell, platelet, nerve cell, epithelial layer, muscle-fiber bundle, and detailed skin section. Separately enlarged cells are not presented as being at the same scale. Native image sizes are respected so small cell illustrations are not enlarged into blurry full-width diagrams.

## Lesson coverage

| The heart: Two connected loops | 1 | Follow the arrows through both loops. Separate the place where blood is pumped from the place where it exchanges gases. |
| Vessels and exchange | 1 | Follow the red route into the fine blood-vessel network and out through the blue route. The separate green branches collect tissue fluid into lymph vessels. |
| Lungs: Exchanging gases | 1 | Follow the oxygen and carbon-dioxide arrows separately. Then follow blood past the air sac without letting the blood route become the air route. |
| Blood: More than one job | 3 | Compare three components with different jobs: oxygen transport, defense, and clotting. Their shapes help distinguish them, but shape alone does not show the full job. |
| Breathing: Changing space and pressure | 1 | Follow the large airway into smaller branches. Air reaches these structures because chest movement changes pressure; the muscle action itself is not drawn here. |
| Preview: Deliveries to every cell | 1 | Find the heart and follow one route toward a branching tissue network. What other organ must exchange gases before blood can keep supplying those tissues? |
| Review: Trace an oxygen delivery | 1 | Trace the arrows through the lung and body loops. Identify where blood changes its gas cargo, then where that cargo can reach body tissues. |
| Brain, spinal cord, and nerves | 1 | Compare the large upper regions with the smaller gray region at the lower back and the pink connecting stalk. Use their positions to organize their cooperating jobs. |
| Hormones: Messages carried in blood | 1 | Find the thyroid beside the windpipe. Connect this location with a message that enters blood; the pituitary and target-cell receptors are not shown. |
| Feedback: Keeping conditions workable | 1 | Find the green sweat gland and the red and blue blood vessels. Connect these structures with the cooling response, then consider why that response decreases as excess warmth falls. |
| Hearing and balance: Turning movement into signals | 1 | Trace sound from the ear canal toward the coiled inner-ear structure. Contrast the coil’s hearing role with the nearby curved balance structures. |
| Vision: From light to information | 1 | Trace light from the curved front toward the back lining. Then locate the nerve leaving the eye: the route changes from light to cellular signals. |
| Preview: Sense, respond, adjust | 2 | Compare the nerve map with the thyroid’s position. Nerve signals follow connected cells; thyroid hormones enter blood and can reach distant responsive cells. |
| Reflexes: A quick protective route | 1 | Find the spinal cord below the brain, then a branching nerve route. Imagine a response beginning through spinal connections while another route carries information upward. |
| Review: Information, messages, feedback | 2 | Compare the two message routes. Use the nerve map for a movement command, then use the thyroid to explain how a hormone can act at a distant target. |
| From mouth to stomach | 1 | Locate the central stomach space and surrounding wall. Connect mixing with muscle in the wall and chemical digestion with substances released into the space. |
| Absorption and the large intestine | 1 | Trace a route from the intestinal space across a thin villus lining toward a transport vessel. Notice how many projections increase the crossing surface. |
| Kidneys: Filtering and keeping useful materials | 1 | Start at each kidney and follow its tube to the bladder, then find the separate outlet. Distinguish urine production from storage. |
| Digestive helpers: Liver, gallbladder, pancreas | 1 | Locate the green gallbladder and its ducts beneath the liver. Follow the bile route toward the intestine rather than sending a bite of food through the liver. |
| Preview: Getting supplies, removing wastes | 2 | Trace the food route in the first picture and the urine route in the second. Notice that the kidneys are not another stop along the food tube. |
| Review: Follow a meal and a waste | 2 | Use the two routes to distinguish stool from urine. Then identify where absorbed nutrients leave the food route and why their later transport requires blood. |
| Water balance: Changing what is kept | 2 | Compare the two views. Skin can lose water through sweat, while kidneys can adjust how much filtered water returns to blood. Which changes would help conserve water? |
| Cells and tissues | 2 | Compare the long extension of the nerve cell with the closely packed cells in the lining. How does each arrangement support a different job? |
| Organs and systems | 2 | Find the stomach in the system overview, then inspect its cutaway. Notice how a single organ has internal structures and also belongs to a larger connected route. |
| Following connections | 1 | Start at the heart in the circulation map and follow a route toward a body tissue. Imagine oxygen being delivered there while a separate nerve pathway controls muscle activity. |
| Start here: A body made of working parts | 2 | Compare the routes in these two maps. One carries blood; the other carries nerve signals. Which route could deliver oxygen, and which could guide a movement? |
| Review: Build a body explanation | 2 | Use the close-up and the organ picture to rebuild the levels: cells form tissue; several tissues contribute to an organ; the organ cooperates in a system. |
| Skin: Barrier, sensing, temperature | 1 | Compare the intact upper boundary, yellow sensory structures, green coiled sweat gland, and red and blue vessels. Connect each with a different skin job. |
| Immune cells and lymph routes | 1 | Follow the vessels toward and away from the node. Distinguish the transport route pictured here from immune-cell encounters occurring inside the node. |
| Early responses and immune memory | 2 | Compare a physical barrier with a defense cell. Which can reduce entry, and which can act after entry? Neither picture alone explains immune memory. |
| Preview: Layers of protection | 2 | Start with the skin’s intact surface, then compare a defense cell. A barrier can reduce entry; an internal cell response helps when material gets past a barrier. |
| Review: A barrier is crossed | 2 | Imagine material crossing the skin barrier. Use the pictures to connect the entry point with transport toward a lymph node where immune cells can encounter carried material. |
| Living bones and joints | 1 | Locate the skull, rib cage, and spine. Compare the elbow’s joint with the shoulder’s: which movements would their different shapes allow? |
| Muscles make tension | 1 | Follow the muscle bundles toward the tendon. Trace how a pull could reach a bone even while the muscle holds a load without visible movement. |
| Movement needs connections | 1 | Find the bones meeting at the elbow. Identify what this cutaway leaves out: the muscle pull and control signal needed to move or steady the joint. |
| Preview: How can a body move? | 2 | Locate the elbow joint in the skeleton, then compare it with the cutaway. The skeleton provides orientation; the close-up reveals where bones meet. |
| Review: Explain a lift | 2 | Use the first picture to locate the meeting of bones. Use the second to connect a muscle’s internal structure with the tendon that transfers its pull. |
| Activity: Matching delivery to demand | 2 | Use the first figure to explain increased supply demand, then trace a delivery route in the second. The pictures show connected structures, not measurements of pulse or oxygen. |
| After a meal: Delivery and repair | 2 | Find the intestine and liver in the digestive overview. Then inspect the pancreas separately: its digestive secretions and blood-borne hormones take different routes. |
| Final connections: Explain, predict, check | 2 | Choose a material entering through one system and reaching another through blood. Use both maps to identify what the pictures show and what your explanation must add. |
| Preview: Explain a whole-body event | 2 | Connect a muscle’s work with blood delivery. Which picture helps explain the pull, and which helps explain how supplies can reach working cells? |
| Final review: Tell the whole story | 2 | Choose one scenario from this review. Use the two maps to trace a material and a separate control signal; name the connection your explanation adds between the pictures. |

The content validator requires a picture in each lesson body or its explicit lesson-image list. Shipped-content tests also check every lesson for image coverage and a visual-reading task. Catalog images alone do not satisfy this requirement.
