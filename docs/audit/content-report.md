## Cells

A **cell** is the basic living unit. Cells are tiny, but they are not all copies of one shape.

- A **muscle cell** develops tension. It can shorten to create movement or hold steady to maintain a position.
- A **nerve cell** has long branches so it can carry a signal from one place to another.
- A **red blood cell** is a small disk that travels easily through narrow vessels.

The shape is a clue to the job.

## Tissues

A **tissue** is a group of similar cells working on the same job. Four broad tissue types provide a useful starting model:

| Tissue | What it does | A place you will meet it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epithelial | Covers and lines surfaces | Skin surface, lining of the mouth and stomach |
| Connective | Supports and connects | Bone, blood, tendons |
| Muscle | Develops tension; moves or holds body parts | Muscles that move bones, the heart |
| Nervous | Carries signals | Brain, spinal cord, nerves |

Later units will not ask you to memorize long lists of rare cell names. They will keep coming back to these four tissues and to the idea that **parts work together**.


**Reflect:** Compare a nerve cell with a muscle cell. How does each cell’s structure help it do a different job?

## From a tissue to an organ

A tissue is a team of similar cells. An **organ** is the next step up: a structure made of **two or more kinds of tissue** whose tissues cooperate on related jobs.

*The digestive overview places the stomach between the food tube from the mouth and the intestines. Food follows the digestive tube rather than passing through every nearby organ. Servier Medical Art, CC BY 4.0. [Source and license](#/credits).*


The stomach is a clear example. It is not just a bag of muscle.

- Epithelial tissue lines the inside.
- Muscle tissue in the wall churns food.
- Nervous tissue tells the muscle when to squeeze.
- Connective tissue, including blood, supports the wall and brings supplies.

The heart, lungs, brain, and kidneys are organs for the same reason. Each one is a structure with a job, built from more than one tissue.

## From organs to systems

An **organ system** is a group of organs that share a larger job.

| System you will study | A few main parts | The shared job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skeletal and muscular | Bones, joints, skeletal muscles | Support the body and move it |
| Circulatory and respiratory | Heart, vessels, lungs, diaphragm | Deliver oxygen and remove carbon dioxide |
| Digestive and urinary | Stomach, intestines, liver, kidneys | Take in fuel and remove many wastes |
| Nervous and endocrine | Brain, nerves, glands | Carry and respond to information |
| Integumentary and immune | Skin, lymph nodes, spleen | Block invaders and respond when they get in |

Systems are useful groupings, not sealed boxes. One organ can contribute to more than one job; the pancreas, for example, helps digestion and sends hormone messages.

The whole **organism** is the living person: all of those systems in one body.

## Keep the connections

The next units look at those systems one at a time. Each unit still belongs to the same body.

## No system is an island

It is useful to study bones, then the heart, then the stomach. That is not how the body runs. **Each system depends on the others.**

### Read the picture

Start at the heart in the circulation map and follow a route toward a body tissue. Imagine oxygen being delivered there while a separate nerve pathway controls muscle activity.

![The central pump connects with routes to many tissues. The red and blue colors distinguish routes with different oxygen levels; real blood is always a shade of red.](images/servier/bloodstream.png)

*The central pump connects with routes to many tissues. The red and blue colors distinguish routes with different oxygen levels; real blood is always a shade of red. Servier Medical Art, CC BY 4.0. [Source and license](#/credits).*


Muscle activity requires energy. Blood brings supplies, including oxygen and nutrients, that support the processes releasing that energy. The lungs load the blood with oxygen. The digestive system is the source of the fuel. Nerves tell the muscle when to pull. Skin and sweat may cool you if the work lasts.

## Follow one ordinary action

Imagine standing up from a chair and walking to the door.

1. **Nervous system.** Your brain and nerves plan the movement and send the signal.
2. **Muscular and skeletal systems.** Muscles pull on bones at the hips, knees, and ankles.
3. **Circulatory and respiratory systems.** Breathing and circulation continue supplying oxygen; they can increase as activity demands rise.
4. **Digestive system, earlier.** The fuel those cells burn came from food you already ate.

None of those steps is optional for long. If the signal, the pull, the oxygen, or the fuel is missing, the walk changes or stops.

## How the course will use this idea

The next units take the systems in turn:

- support and movement
- circulation and breathing
- fuel and waste
- control and coordination
- protection and defense

Each unit ends by pointing toward the next dependency. The last unit puts the pieces back into everyday scenes: activity, a meal, recovery, and responding to a warning. The question to keep is always the same. **How does this part serve the whole body?**

Stand up, throw a ball, or take a breath. Bones are involved every time, and not only as a frame to hang muscles on. ## Joints

Bones meet at **joints**. They do not all move the same amount.

<details><summary>Lesson text</summary>

## Stable does not mean motionless
Body cells work best within certain ranges of temperature, water balance, and other conditions. **Homeostasis** means maintaining workable internal conditions despite changes inside and outside the body. It does not mean every measurement stays at one exact number.

A feedback process connects detection, control, and response. Sensors detect a change. Control signals influence responding tissues. The response changes the original condition, which affects later signals.

## Defense has layers
Barriers such as skin and mucus reduce entry. If microbes cross them, **innate defenses** can respond quickly to general signs of trouble. Some cells take in microbes and debris. Inflammation changes local blood flow and vessel leakage, helping defense cells and materials reach an affected area.

Redness and swelling can be part of that response. They are not proof of one specific cause, and inflammation can also occur after injury without infection.

## A more targeted response
**Adaptive defenses** recognize particular targets. Some cells help make antibodies, proteins that bind particular targets. Others help coordinate responses or act against infected cells.

Early defenses help shape targeted responses, and targeted responses can help other defense cells work. Memory is useful but does not guarantee that every future exposure causes no illness.

## More work changes demand
During more intense activity, muscle cells use energy faster. Many of their energy-releasing processes use oxygen and produce carbon dioxide and heat. Circulation and breathing help match delivery and removal to the changed demand.

Heart rate usually rises, and the heart can pump more blood per minute. Blood flow is redistributed among tissues. Breathing often becomes faster, deeper, or both, supporting gas exchange.
